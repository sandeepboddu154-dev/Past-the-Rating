## 01 · The Atrium  (0:43)

**S04 · 0:45–1:09** · [VISUAL: `atrium_build`]

> The Hyatt Regency Kansas City opened in July 1980. Its centrepiece was a vast atrium, a glass-roofed lobby several storeys high. And crossing it, in mid-air, were three suspended walkways. The second-floor walkway hung directly beneath the one on the fourth floor. The third-floor walkway ran on its own, off to one side.

**S05 · 1:09–1:24** · [VISUAL: `atrium_rods`]

> The walkways didn't rest on columns. They hung from the roof on slender steel rods, so they seemed to float across the room. People loved them. From up there, you could look straight down onto the lobby floor.

**S06 · 1:24–1:39** · [VISUAL: `atrium_crowd`]

> On Friday evenings that summer, the hotel held a tea dance in the atrium. On July the seventeenth, around sixteen hundred people came. Dozens of them stood on the walkways, watching the dancers below.

## 02 · The Rating  (1:39)

**S07 · 1:42–1:48** · [VISUAL: `joint_zoom`]

> To understand what happened next, you only need to understand one joint.

**S08 · 1:48–2:13** · [VISUAL: `boxbeam_exploded`]

> Each walkway was carried by cross-beams called box beams. Each box beam was made from two C-shaped steel channels, welded edge to edge to form a hollow box. A steel rod passed through a hole in that box. Underneath, a nut and a washer held the beam up. All the weight of that section of walkway pressed down onto that one nut.

**S09 · 2:13–2:32** · [VISUAL: `original_rod`]

> The original design was simple. One long, continuous rod would run from the roof, through the fourth-floor walkway, and straight on down to the second-floor walkway. Each nut carried only one walkway. The rod itself carried both, and steel rods are very good at that.

**S10 · 2:32–2:51** · [VISUAL: `bar_sixty`]

> But even this design had a problem. When federal investigators later analysed it, they concluded that the connection would have reached only about sixty percent of the capacity the Kansas City building code expected. Before anything changed, it was already under-rated.

## 03 · The Change  (2:51)

**S11 · 2:53–3:08** · [VISUAL: `long_thread`]

> A single continuous rod has a practical problem. It's long, and the nut for the fourth floor has to sit somewhere in the middle of it. Getting it there, and building the walkway around it, is awkward work.

**S12 · 3:08–3:29** · [VISUAL: `split_compare`]

> So during construction, the detail was changed. Instead of one rod, there would be two. The first ran from the roof down to the fourth-floor box beam. A second, separate rod hung from that same box beam, down to the second-floor walkway. On paper, the two arrangements look almost identical.

**S13 · 3:29–3:44** · [VISUAL: `load_flow`]

> But follow the load. The second-floor walkway no longer hangs from the roof. It hangs from the fourth-floor walkway. So the nut under the fourth-floor box beam now carries the fourth floor, and everything hanging beneath it.

**S14 · 3:44–4:04** · [VISUAL: `climbers`]

> Picture two climbers on a rope. Each one holds the rope, and each grip carries one person. Now the lower climber lets go, and holds on to the upper climber's ankles instead. Nothing about the rope has changed. But the upper climber's grip is now holding two people.

**S15 · 4:04–4:13** · [VISUAL: `bar_double`]

> That was the fourth-floor connection. A joint that was already under-rated was now being asked to carry roughly twice the load.

**S16 · 4:13–4:42** · [VISUAL: `paper_trail`]

> So how did a change like that get through? The steel fabricator proposed the two-rod detail and prepared the shop drawings. Those drawings went back to the structural engineers, and returned with their review stamp. What was said along the way, including a disputed phone call, became one of the most argued-over questions in engineering ethics. What the investigations made clear is that the connection was never checked against the doubled load.

**S17 · 4:42–5:01** · [VISUAL: `roof_warning`]

> And there had been a warning sign. While the hotel was still being built, part of the atrium roof collapsed. Nobody was killed. The engineers investigated, and assured the owner of the atrium's safety. The flaw in the walkway connections was not caught.

## 04 · The Day  (5:01)

**S18 · 5:03–5:22** · [VISUAL: `the_day`]

> Friday, July the seventeenth, 1981. The atrium is full. There's music, and there are people on the floor, and on the walkways above. The fourth-floor walkway is carrying its own crowd, and the entire second-floor walkway below it, with its crowd too.

**S19 · 5:22–5:41** · [VISUAL: `failure`]

> At about five past seven in the evening, at the fourth-floor box beams, the welded seams split. The nuts and washers pulled straight through the steel. The fourth-floor walkway dropped onto the second-floor walkway, and both fell onto the crowded lobby below.

**S20 · 5:41–6:01** · [VISUAL: `memorial`]

> One hundred and fourteen people died. Two hundred and sixteen were injured. Rescuers worked through the night to reach those trapped beneath the wreckage. It was the deadliest structural collapse in American history, until 2001.

## 05 · The Investigation  (6:01)

**S21 · 6:04–6:30** · [VISUAL: `investigation`]

> The National Bureau of Standards, known today as NIST, led the federal investigation. Its engineers recovered the hanger rods and box beams, and load-tested matching connections in the laboratory. Their conclusion was stark. The load on the connection at the moment of collapse was only about thirty-one percent of what a connection designed to the Kansas City code should have been able to hold.

**S22 · 6:30–6:41** · [VISUAL: `capacity_drop`]

> In other words, this was not a structure pushed past its rating by a freak event. Its rating had been quietly cut, long before the crowd ever arrived.

## 06 · Who Paid  (6:41)

**S23 · 6:44–7:13** · [VISUAL: `ledger`]

> So who decided, and who paid? The change made one detail simpler to fabricate and install. That was the entire upside. The downside was a wave of lawsuits from victims and their families. Missouri's licensing authorities found the engineer of record and the project engineer grossly negligent. Both lost their engineering licences in Missouri, and their firm lost its certificate to practise engineering in the state.

**S24 · 7:13–7:26** · [VISUAL: `stamp`]

> Engineering ethics courses still debate how the responsibility should have been shared. But the lesson the profession took was blunt. If your stamp is on it, it's yours.

## 07 · What Changed  (7:26)

**S25 · 7:29–7:58** · [VISUAL: `what_changed`]

> After Kansas City, the industry tightened how connection designs and shop drawings are reviewed. Today, a change like that two-rod detail is supposed to trigger a fresh calculation, not just a stamp. The Hyatt Regency is now taught in engineering ethics classes across the United States. Not because the physics is complicated. It's one rod, two nuts, and a load that doubled. It's taught because the number was always there to be checked.

**S26 · 7:58–8:15** · [VISUAL: `closing`]

> Every structure around you was built to carry a number. Most of the time, there's a margin on top of it that you'll never see. The question is always the same. How far past the rating can it really go?
