## Why this is the flagship
- It's *the* textbook "past the rating" story: a connection that never met its rated load, then had its load doubled by a small change.

- It carries the Money Line (fabrication convenience vs. $100M+ consequences, licences revoked), which is advertiser-friendlier than gore-driven disaster content.
- Search demand is evergreen (taught in every engineering ethics course, so students search it every semester).
- **Primary visual source is public domain:** the US National Bureau of Standards investigation report (NBS BSS 143, 1982) is a US-government work.

## Full script (voiceover)
*Formatting: **[VISUAL]** cues in brackets; `ON-SCREEN` text in code. Read at ~150 wpm; "…" marks a deliberate pause.*

### 0:00 — Cold open: The moment
**[VISUAL]** Black. A single steel rod drawn as a white line. A nut threads onto it. Rating Bar appears: `CONNECTION RATED: 100%` → fill stops at `~30%` in orange.

> Every piece of a building is designed to carry a number.
> A beam, a bolt, a weld… each one has a load it's rated for, with a margin on top in case the world is heavier than we expected.
> On a Friday evening in July 1981, in a hotel in Kansas City, one small connection was holding up two walkways full of people.
> It had never been strong enough to meet the building code.
> And months earlier, a change on a drawing had doubled what it was being asked to carry.
> At 7:05 PM, it reached its limit.

**[VISUAL]** Hard cut to silence. Title card: `PAST THE RATING` — `The Drawing Change That Doubled the Load`.

### 0:50 — The atrium
**[VISUAL]** Clean 3D-style isometric line drawing of a hotel atrium; three skywalks span it; labels draw on.

> The Hyatt Regency Kansas City opened in July 1980. Its centrepiece was a huge open atrium, and crossing it, high in the air, were three suspended skywalks.
> The second-floor walkway hung directly below the fourth-floor walkway. The third-floor walkway ran separately, off to one side.
> And these walkways didn't rest on columns. They hung from the roof on thin steel rods, so they seemed to float across the lobby.
> It was a striking piece of architecture. Guests loved to stand on the walkways and look down at the crowds below.
> Which is exactly what they were doing on the evening of July 17th, 1981.

**[VISUAL]** Floor plan: walkway 4 above walkway 2 highlighted.

> Every Friday that summer, the hotel held a tea dance in the atrium. That night, by most estimates, around 1,600 people were in the building for it. Dozens stood on the walkways, watching the band and the dancers below.

### 2:40 — The rating
**[VISUAL]** Zoom into one hanger point. Exploded diagram: rod, nut, washer, box beam (two C-shaped steel channels welded toe to toe).

> To understand what happened, you only need to understand one joint.
> Each walkway was carried by long cross-beams called box beams. And each box beam was made from two C-shaped steel channels, welded edge to edge to form a hollow box.
> A steel rod passed through a hole in that box beam. Underneath, a nut and washer held the beam up. The whole weight of that section of walkway pressed down onto that nut.
> The original design was elegant. One single, continuous rod would run from the roof, through the fourth-floor walkway, and straight on down through the second-floor walkway.

**[VISUAL]** Animation: continuous rod, two nuts. Each nut glows with load `P`.

> In that design, each nut carries just one walkway. The nut under the fourth floor carries the fourth floor. The nut under the second floor carries the second floor. The rod itself carries both, but steel rods are strong in tension. That's fine.
> But even this original design had a problem. When investigators later checked it, they found the connection would only have carried around sixty percent of the load the Kansas City building code required.

`ORIGINAL DESIGN: ≈ 60% OF CODE`

> It was under-rated before anything changed.

### 4:20 — The change
**[VISUAL]** A shop drawing (stylised, not a real document) slides in. The single long rod is erased and replaced by two shorter rods.

> Here's the practical problem with one continuous rod: it's long, and the nut for the fourth-floor walkway has to sit in the middle of it. To get it there, you'd have to thread the nut along many feet of rod. It's awkward to fabricate and awkward to install.
> So during construction the detail was changed. Instead of one rod, there would be two.
> The first rod would run from the roof down to the fourth-floor box beam. A second, separate rod would hang from that same fourth-floor box beam, and go down to the second-floor walkway.
> On paper, the two drawings look almost identical. Same rods, same nuts, same walkways.

**[VISUAL]** Side-by-side: ORIGINAL vs AS BUILT. Pause. Then load arrows animate.

> But follow the load.
> In the new arrangement, the second-floor walkway no longer hangs from the roof rod. It hangs from the fourth-floor walkway.
> So the nut under the fourth-floor box beam now carries the fourth floor… *and* everything hanging below it.

`AS BUILT: 2 × LOAD ON ONE CONNECTION`

> Here's a simpler way to see it. Picture two climbers on one rope. Each grips the rope, and each hand holds one person. Now the lower climber lets go of the rope and grabs the upper climber's ankles instead. The rope hasn't changed. But the upper climber's grip is now holding two people.
> That was the fourth-floor connection. A joint that already met only around sixty percent of code was now being asked to carry roughly twice the load.
> Investigators later estimated that, as built, it could hold only about thirty percent of what the code required.

**[VISUAL]** Rating Bar: tick at 100% (`CODE`), fill to 30%, orange.

`AS BUILT: ≈ 30% OF CODE`

> How did a change like that get through?
> The steel fabricator proposed the two-rod detail and produced the shop drawings. Those drawings went back to the structural engineering firm and came back bearing its review stamp. What was said along the way, including whether a phone call amounted to approval, became one of the most argued-over questions in engineering ethics. What no one disputes is that nobody ran the calculation that would have shown the load had doubled.

**[VISUAL]** Insert: timeline card `OCT 1979 — Part of the atrium roof collapses during construction (no deaths).`

> And there had been a warning. In October 1979, during construction, part of the atrium's roof collapsed. Nobody was hurt. The connections were supposed to be re-examined. The walkway hangers were not caught.

### 6:40 — The day
**[VISUAL]** Slow, dim reconstruction of the atrium (line art, no people's faces). The band stage glows. Clock: `7:05 PM`.

> Friday, July 17th, 1981. The atrium is full. Music. Crowds on the floor and crowds on the walkways above.
> The fourth-floor walkway is carrying its people, and the second-floor walkway beneath it, and all of its people too.
> At 7:05 PM, at the fourth-floor box beams, the welded seams split open. The nuts and washers pulled straight through the steel.

**[VISUAL]** The diagram: nut pulls through the box-beam seam (orange). Then the graphic simply cuts to black. **No depiction of falling people.**

> The fourth-floor walkway fell onto the second-floor walkway, and both fell onto the crowded lobby below.
> One hundred and fourteen people died. More than two hundred were injured.

**[VISUAL]** Black screen with white text for 4 seconds: `114 people lost their lives. More than 200 were injured.` Silence.

> Rescuers worked through the night. Hospitals across Kansas City filled. It remained the deadliest structural collapse in American history until 2001.

### 8:30 — The investigation
**[VISUAL]** Cover of NBS BSS 143 (public domain). Test-rig diagram.

> The federal National Bureau of Standards, now NIST, investigated. They recovered the hanger rods and box beams and load-tested matching connections in the lab.
> Their findings were stark. The walkways had not been overloaded by some extraordinary crowd. The connection itself was the failure. As designed, it fell short of the code. As built, with the load doubled, it fell far short.
> In other words, this wasn't a structure that went past its rating on a freak day. Its rating had been quietly cut long before the crowd ever arrived.

### 10:00 — The Money Line
**[VISUAL]** Ledger-style graphics. Steel/orange.

> So who decided, and who paid?
> The design change saved fabrication time and effort on a few connections. That was the entire upside.
> The downside became one of the largest liability cases in American construction history. Lawsuits followed from victims and families, and settlements and judgments ran to well over one hundred million dollars.
> In 1985, a Missouri commission found the engineer of record and the project engineer responsible for gross negligence and unprofessional conduct. Both lost their engineering licences in Missouri. Their firm lost its certificate.
> The steel fabricator was not held professionally liable in that ruling. Engineering ethics courses still argue about how responsibility should be split.
> But the lesson the profession took was blunt. If your stamp is on it, it's yours.

`THE SAVING: a few hours of fabrication.` → `THE COST: 114 lives. $100M+ in claims. Two careers.`

### 11:20 — What changed
**[VISUAL]** Checklist animation.

> After Kansas City, the industry tightened how connections and shop drawings are handled. Engineering societies issued guidance that the engineer of record is responsible for the overall structure, including reviewing changes to connection design. Today, a substitution like that two-rod detail is supposed to trigger a fresh calculation, not just a stamp.
> The Hyatt Regency is now taught in nearly every engineering ethics class in the United States. Not because the physics is complicated. It's one rod, two nuts, and a load that doubled.
> It's taught because the number was always there to be checked.

**[VISUAL]** Final Rating Bar: `RATED 100%` tick, fill at 30%, then the bar resets to empty.

> Every structure around you was built to carry a number. Most of the time, there's a margin on top of it that you'll never see.
> The question is always the same.
> How far past the rating can it really go?

**[VISUAL]** Logo sting (orange slash cuts the square, signature creak + tink). End screen 20 s.
